**go-tui adds interactivity to your CLI without changing its architecture.** Call typed [prompts](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/input/), [selections](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/dropdowns/), [tables](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/table/) and [progress bars](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/progressbar/) from ordinary Go functions; keep your [contexts](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/options/#context) and application control flow. Prompts and progress share a [terminal coordinator](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/options/#stderr) with logs routed through `tui.Stderr()`, so you can combine them without stitching together [competing renderers](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/alternatives/#prompts-progress-and-logs-together). The library directly depends only on `golang.org/x`.

![demo](site/static/demos/misc/everything-and-logs.gif)

**The same command works at a terminal and in automation.** Non-TTY [defaults](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/options/#defaults-without-a-terminal) are a first-class contract: explicit answers are [validated](https://nfx.github.io/go-tui/options/#input-validation-and-feedback) and returned without reading input or printing prompts, so CI jobs and redirected commands never wait for an interactive answer. Missing or invalid defaults fail instead of opening a prompt; progress falls back to structured `slog` records. That combination of typed answers, coordinated output and predictable batch behavior makes go-tui a strong fit for operational CLIs. Rich interactive applications with grids, pages, trees and navigable views are outside its scope; see [Alternatives](site/content/alternatives.md) for the tradeoffs. See the [getting started guide](site/content/getting-started.md).
